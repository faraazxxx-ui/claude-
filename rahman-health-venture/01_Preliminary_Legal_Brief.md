## PART III — THE BUSINESS, STATED IN OPERATIVE TERMS

**One sentence:** A physician-owned New York primary care practice that sells time rather than volume, priced for the middle market, delivered substantially in the home, capitalized without interest-bearing debt, and paired with a separate U.S. charitable entity that funds care for patients who cannot pay.

### The clinical thesis
Three years of residency practice in Broome County — among the poorest catchment areas in New York — produced one durable finding: **unhurried physician time outperforms incremental diagnostic testing**, both in outcome and in cost. Two corollaries drive the model:

- **Continuity suppresses litigation.** I restructured my own residency clinic panel so I saw the same patients on a recurring cycle rather than a new patient each session. Within six months I held a stable panel with genuine rapport — including a family that took a full day off work to travel in together. The mechanism is well understood in the malpractice literature and inverts the usual defensive-medicine incentive: **relationship is a risk control, not a soft benefit.** Underwriters should price it.
- **Setting distorts measurement.** White-coat hypertension is the accepted, uncontroversial case of a clinical measurement corrupted by the clinical environment. It is not plausible that the effect is confined to blood pressure. Home-based assessment is a *data quality* intervention before it is a comfort intervention.

### The market
**Target: middle-income working households, not the indigent.** This is deliberate and I want it stated plainly, because it is the point most likely to be misread as either charity or luxury concierge medicine. It is neither. The binding constraint on a working family is not money, it is **time and access**. A household that can absorb $200–$300 for an unhurried hour, delivered at home, outside working hours, is a household currently receiving worse care than it can afford — because insurance networks ration by panel availability, not by price. That gap is the business.

**On free markets:** when an insurer dictates which physicians a patient may see, there is no consumer choice in any meaningful sense. The venture sells choice back.

### Supply-side arbitrage
The United States is short primary care physicians. Two underused pools:

1. **Dual-certified specialists.** Every U.S. cardiologist, nephrologist, endocrinologist and gastroenterologist completed internal medicine first and most retain board certification. They are a latent, fully-credentialed primary care workforce, available in marginal hours at a marginal price, with no new training pipeline required.
2. **The Indian physician diaspora and Indian training pipeline.** India produces a physician surplus and *mandates* rural service in training — Indian ophthalmology and plastic surgery services have for decades run charitable rural outreach, in some cases chartering aircraft to reach it. The professional norm there treats service as status, not as sacrifice. That is the workforce culture this venture needs, and it is already the culture that built the American medical infrastructure over the last fifty years.

### Capital and cost structure
- **No interest-bearing debt.** Consequence: equity, revenue-based, or asset-backed (lease/cost-plus) structures only. See Appendix A.
- **Charity care sits in a separate charitable entity**, not in the operating practice — for reasons set out in Part V.C.
- **Generic pharmaceutical sourcing** as a patient cost-reduction lever, on a hard clock — Part IV, Workstream 6.

### Workstream 3 — The clinical entity: CPOM-compliant formation
**PRIORITY: HIGH. Blocks all revenue.**

**The problem I did not know I had.** New York enforces the corporate practice of medicine doctrine strictly. Only NY-licensed physicians may own a medical practice. It must be a **Professional Service Corporation (PC)** or **PLLC**, and it requires a **Certificate of Authority from NYSED** before Department of State filing. Recent New York legislation tightened this further: licensed physicians must hold not only ownership but **majority board control and all voting shares**, and non-physician management organizations are barred from exercising governance over the professional entity.

**Direct consequence for my partners:** my World Bank–affiliated partner and my Dubai-based technology partner **cannot hold equity in the clinical entity.** I need counsel to build the standard two-entity structure:

| Entity | Ownership | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Clinical PC/PLLC** | 100% NY-licensed physicians (me, and physician partners only) | Employs clinicians. Holds the payor and licensure relationships. Bills for care. All clinical decisions. |
| **MSO (LLC or C-corp)** | Open to non-physician partners and outside capital | Administration, technology, billing, HR, marketing, real estate, capital. Contracts with the PC on a **fixed, fair-market-value, pre-agreed fee** — *not* a percentage of practice revenue. |

**The fee-splitting trap:** New York prohibits fee-splitting between a professional entity and a non-licensed entity. Percentage-of-revenue MSO compensation is generally treated as prohibited fee-splitting. Permitted structures are fixed fee, or cost-plus-reasonable-margin, documented in writing before services are rendered, supported by an FMV analysis. **This constrains how my partners can be economically rewarded and I need it solved early, not discovered at diligence.** N.Y. Educ. Law § 6509-a and N.Y. Pub. Health Law § 238-a are the operative provisions counsel should work from.

### Workstream 4 — Revenue model: the insurance-law problem
**PRIORITY: HIGH. Determines whether the model is lawful as designed.**

**This is the finding I most want counsel to confirm or refute, because it changes the product.**

**New York has not enacted a direct primary care safe-harbor statute.** Roughly thirty states have; New York has not. A recurring flat membership or retainer fee, paid in advance, in exchange for an undefined future quantity of care, is exposed to being characterized by the **Department of Financial Services** as **the doing of an insurance business** under N.Y. Insurance Law § 1102 — because the practice would be accepting morbidity risk for a fixed premium. That is unauthorized insurance, and it is not a technical foot-fault.

**Two consequences, one of them favorable:**

1. **My instinct to price by the hour was correct, for a reason I did not know.** Fee-for-service at $200–$300 per hour transfers no morbidity risk and raises no § 1102 issue. **Counsel should treat hourly/per-encounter fee-for-service as the default architecture and treat any membership or subscription layer as requiring affirmative DFS analysis before launch.** New York practices operating retainer models appear to manage this by keeping per-visit charges below the periodic fee; I want that mechanism examined rather than copied.
2. **This converges with a religious constraint.** The Islamic prohibition on ***gharar*** — excessive uncertainty in the subject matter of a contract — independently disfavors selling an undefined future quantity of care for a fixed advance premium. Shariah analysis and New York insurance law reach the **same** structural answer. Appendix A develops this; it is the strongest bridge argument in the memo and I would lead with it.

**Also required:**
- **Medicare opt-out.** If I privately contract with Medicare-eligible patients for covered services, I must formally opt out under § 1802(b) via affidavit on a two-year cycle and use compliant private contracts. Balance-billing a Medicare beneficiary without a valid opt-out is a serious violation.
- **The Medicare/Medicaid tension.** I want to reduce public program burden and work with New York State. But entering federal program space activates the **federal Anti-Kickback Statute** (42 U.S.C. § 1320a-7b(b)), **Stark**, and the civil monetary penalty rules — exposure that a pure cash-pay practice largely avoids. Counsel should advise on which side of that line each service line sits, and I would prefer the charitable entity, not the PC, carry anything that touches public programs.
- **The immigration collision.** Conrad 30 sponsorship requires accepting all patients regardless of ability to pay, including Medicare, Medicaid and the uninsured. The physician NIW requires five years of full-time HPSA/MUA service. **Both are in direct tension with a middle-market cash-pay practice.** I do not want this papered over. My proposed resolution: my personal clinical service satisfies the HPSA/underserved obligation through the charitable entity or an employed underserved-site position, while the cash-pay PC is built and staffed by physicians *not* under a service obligation. Counsel should pressure-test whether that separation survives both USCIS and CPOM scrutiny.

### Workstream 5 — The Rahman Foundation
**PRIORITY: HIGH — and my stated plan for it was wrong.**

**Objective:** a vehicle that receives *zakat* and general charitable contributions, and funds care for patients the PC cannot serve at market rates.

**Why Ireland fails for this purpose.** IRC **§ 170(c)(2)(A)** allows a charitable deduction only for contributions to organizations **created or organized in the United States** or its possessions. The narrow treaty exceptions cover certain **Canadian, Mexican, and Israeli** organizations. **Ireland is not among them.** An Irish-incorporated Rahman Foundation would give U.S. donors **no** charitable deduction — which defeats the entire purpose of an entity whose core function is soliciting zakat from U.S.-resident Muslim physicians. My reasoning here imported tax-domicile logic from operating-company planning, where it can make sense, into charitable planning, where it is simply wrong.

**Recommended instead:**
- **Domestic 501(c)(3)** as the donation-receiving entity, with New York Charities Bureau registration (Art. 7-A) and a CHAR500 filing obligation.
- Determine **public charity versus private foundation** classification early — the § 4940–4945 excise regime, self-dealing rules, and 5% distribution requirement change the design materially, and my family's involvement raises disqualified-person issues under § 4946.
- If there is a genuine non-U.S. operational reason for an Irish entity, use an **"American Friends of"** structure or a donor-advised fund with expenditure responsibility — not a bare foreign parent.
- **Zakat-specific design:** zakat is religiously restricted as to beneficiary class and, on the classical view, prioritizes the immediate locality. Counsel should structure this as a **restricted fund** with a documented eligibility determination process and a defined geographic service area, and confirm the beneficiary class remains an indefinite charitable class for § 501(c)(3) purposes. Religious-basis charity is permissible; an impermissibly narrow or private-benefit class is not.
- **Charity care cannot sit in the for-profit PC.** Forgone revenue is not a deduction; § 170 requires a transfer to a qualified organization. This is the structural reason the two-entity model is not optional.

### Workstream 6 — Indian generic pharmaceutical sourcing
**PRIORITY: URGENT ON TIMING, and the plan as I described it is a federal crime. Both things are true.**

**What is unlawful.** Importing drugs not approved by FDA is prohibited regardless of approval status abroad — a drug lawfully sold in India is, in the United States, an unapproved new drug. Personal-use importation is *enforcement discretion*, not legality, and provides no basis for a commercial operation. The FDCA § 804 importation pathway is open only to **Canada**. Prosecutions of unlicensed wholesalers distributing unapproved foreign drugs are routine and carry prison sentences. **"Import cheaper non-American insulin and sell it" is not a business plan I can be given; counsel should say so bluntly and I will accept it.**

**What is lawful, and is in fact how the market already works.** Indian manufacturers already supply a large share of U.S. generic volume — lawfully — because they hold **FDA-approved ANDAs** and operate FDA-registered, inspected facilities. The compliant structure:
- Source **only** from Indian manufacturers holding existing U.S. ANDAs for the specific presentation.
- Obtain a **New York wholesale drug distributor license** (and any other state where we distribute), and comply with **DSCSA** track-and-trace.
- Consider a **503B outsourcing facility** relationship for compounded presentations, or straightforward group-purchasing to compress acquisition cost.

**The clock, which changes the strategic conclusion entirely.** Under the 2026 U.S. tariff framework: patented pharmaceuticals and their ingredients carry a **100% tariff** as announced April 2, 2026, while **generics and biosimilars are excluded for now** — specifically, **zero tariff on imported generics for two years beginning August 1, 2026, then 100% for one year, then 200% thereafter.**

**Read that schedule for what it is: an onshoring mandate with a two-year grace period.** The arbitrage window on *importing* Indian generics runs roughly **August 2026 to August 2028** and then inverts violently. Therefore:
- Treat importing as a **bridge**, deliberately time-boxed, not as a durable line of business.
- The durable play the tariff schedule actually rewards is **U.S.-sited manufacturing or fill-finish**, or a joint venture with an Indian ANDA holder that onshores capacity. If we are serious about the pharmaceutical leg, that is the entity to build, and 2026–2027 is when to build it.
- Counsel should diligence the February 2026 U.S.–India trade understanding for whatever generics-specific carve-out was actually negotiated, since press reporting and the operative instrument may differ.

## PART V — THREE PREMISES OF MINE THAT REQUIRE CORRECTION

I am stating these against my own prior position so counsel does not have to spend the first meeting disagreeing with me.

### A. The settlement will not be taxed at capital gains rates, and "reinvesting it" does not defer anything
**What I believed:** that by directing the settlement into my company rather than taking it as a paycheck, I would pay only capital gains tax.

**Why that is wrong.** Employment discrimination, harassment, and wrongful termination recoveries are **ordinary income**, taxed at rates up to 37% federally plus New York State and City. Back pay and lost wages are ordinary income; punitive damages are always taxable; emotional distress is taxable unless attributable to physical injury. There is no capital gains rate here to reach for, because there is no capital asset being sold. Nor does contributing proceeds to a company you own defer the tax — the § 1400Z-2 opportunity zone deferral applies **only to capital gains**, and there is no like-kind or rollover provision for ordinary income. Contributing after-tax cash to my own entity is an investment decision, not a tax event that helps me.

**The better prize, which is larger than the one I was chasing.** IRC **§ 104(a)(2)** excludes from gross income damages received on account of **personal physical injuries or physical sickness** — an exclusion, at 0%, not a preferential rate. And once there is a qualifying physical injury, emotional distress damages *attributable to* it become excludable as well.

**I have an unusually strong physical-injury record:** Brugada Syndrome; a six-hour cardiac procedure with ICD implantation in February 2024; post-operative infection requiring a PICC line and intravenous vancomycin, during which I was required to continue working; loss of consciousness at work with ICU admission.

**But the pleadings may not currently support the allocation.** The filed counts are Title VI disparate treatment, tortious interference, breach of contract, and injunctive relief. Under the origin-of-the-claim doctrine, the IRS looks to the nature of the claim settled — and a complaint pleading no physical injury tort is weak scaffolding for a physical injury allocation, however real the injuries. Allocations are scrutinized; they are respected when the pleadings, the damages record, and the agreement language corroborate each other.

**The convergence counsel should act on.** The malicious prosecution statute of limitations runs approximately **September 2026** — one year from the August 2025 acquittal — so an amendment is already necessary within weeks to preserve that claim. **The same amendment can add the physical-injury-bearing counts that make a § 104(a)(2) allocation defensible.** One filing, two independent payoffs, on a deadline roughly six weeks out. This is the highest-leverage item in this entire memo and it belongs in front of Mr. Drazen immediately.

### B. Property in Manhattan does not produce an EB-2, and my mother and sister cannot derive from my petition
**What I believed:** that reinvesting settlement funds into Manhattan property would support EB-2 visas allowing my mother and sister to settle, keeping the money inside the U.S. economy rather than repatriating it to Dubai.

**Two independent errors.**

1. **Wrong visa category.** EB-2 is an **employment-based** classification resting on advanced degree or exceptional ability. Investment is **EB-5**, which requires $800,000 (TEA) or $1,050,000 (non-TEA) at risk in an enterprise creating **10 full-time U.S. jobs**. A passive condominium purchase creates no qualifying jobs and does not support EB-5. Buying Manhattan real estate confers no immigration benefit at all, in either category.
2. **Wrong beneficiaries.** Derivative status on an employment-based petition extends only to a **spouse and unmarried children under 21**. **Parents and siblings never derive.** A parent immigrates as IR-5, which requires the petitioner to be a **U.S. citizen** — not a permanent resident. A sibling immigrates in **F4**, which also requires a citizen petitioner and carries a backlog conventionally measured in **fifteen-plus years**. My petition, whatever its category, cannot carry my mother or my sister.

**The reframe, which serves the actual objective better.** My underlying goal is (i) permanent status for me, (ii) security for my mother and sister in the U.S., and (iii) keeping family capital inside the U.S. economy. Those decouple cleanly:

- **For me:** the **physician NIW** under 8 C.F.R. § 204.12 — EB-2 with no employer and no labor certification, purchased with five years of clinical service in an underserved area. I am, on the facts, close to an archetypal candidate. My J-1 § 212(e) obligation must be resolved first.
- **For my mother and sister:** make **my mother the EB-5 principal investor** in her own right, using family capital. My sister may then derive from *my mother's* petition if she is unmarried and under 21; if she is not, she needs an independent basis and counsel should say so now rather than later. **Filing before January 1, 2027 may preserve roughly $100,000** against the scheduled inflation adjustment.
- **For the capital:** an EB-5-qualifying, job-creating investment keeps the money in the U.S. economy *and* generates the immigration benefit — which passive real estate does not. The venture in this memo is itself a candidate job-creating enterprise, though counsel must assess whether family-member investment into my own entity survives EB-5 at-risk and source-of-funds scrutiny.

### C. Charity care by a for-profit practice generates no tax write-off
**What I believed:** that treating patients who cannot pay, and reducing the public program burden, would be a tax write-off for the business.

**Why that is wrong.** Forgone revenue is not a deduction. § 170 requires a transfer of money or property to a **qualified organization**; declining to bill a patient is not such a transfer. A for-profit PC that provides free care simply earns less.

**What actually produces the deduction:** the PC (or I personally, or third-party donors) contributes to the **domestic 501(c)(3)**, and the charitable entity funds the care. That is deductible, and it is the structural reason the Foundation is load-bearing rather than sentimental. It also keeps the public-program and AKS exposure in a separate entity from the cash-pay practice — the same separation that Workstream 4 needs for independent reasons.

## APPENDIX A — TRANSLATION TABLE: ISLAMIC PRINCIPLE → SECULAR LEGAL AND COMMERCIAL FORM

The purpose of this table is narrow and practical. The associate receiving this brief will likely recognize the left column. **The partners reviewing it will read the left column as religious particularity and the right column as ordinary commercial structuring — and the right column is a faithful statement of the same content.** Nothing is being disguised. Each right-hand entry is the doctrine's operative consequence.

| Islamic principle | Operative content | Secular legal / commercial form |
|---|---|---|
| ***Zakāt*** — obligatory annual almsgiving, 2.5% of qualifying wealth | Non-discretionary, formula-based, recurring transfer | **Covenanted charitable commitment**: a fixed-percentage contractual pledge of profits or qualifying assets to a restricted charitable fund. Structurally identical to a 1%-for-the-Planet pledge or a benefit-corporation commitment, and enforceable the same way. |
| ***Bayt al-Māl*** — the public treasury that collected and disbursed zakat | Pooled fund, defined beneficiary class, fiduciary administration, published accounts | **Domestic 501(c)(3) restricted fund** with a written eligibility policy, an independent board, and Charities Bureau reporting. |
| **The census requirement** — zakat may not be disbursed until the community's needs have actually been enumerated | Evidence before expenditure; no disbursement on assumption | **Community Health Needs Assessment.** Not a novelty — it is what § 501(r) already requires of nonprofit hospitals. Framed correctly, this is a compliance asset, not a religious import. |
| **Locality-first rule** — zakat is owed to one's own neighborhood before anywhere else; distant need is addressed only once local need is met | Mandatory geographic prioritization | **Defined catchment area / geographic service-area restriction.** Maps directly onto HRSA HPSA and MUA designation, which is also the currency of the physician NIW and Conrad 30. The religious rule and the immigration statute want the same behavior. |
| ***Riba*** — prohibition on interest | No interest-bearing debt in the capital stack, on either side | **Equity, revenue-share, lease, and cost-plus financing only.** Reads to a partner as a conservative, covenant-light balance sheet. Practically: *murabaha* is cost-plus installment sale, *ijara* is a lease, *mushāraka* is a joint venture. All three have exact conventional analogues. |
| ***Gharar*** — prohibition on excessive contractual uncertainty | You may not sell an undefined future quantity for a fixed present price | **The strongest argument in this brief.** It independently forbids exactly the flat-fee-for-unlimited-care structure that New York Insurance Law § 1102 would treat as unauthorized insurance. Shariah and DFS reach the same answer: **price per encounter, not per period.** A religious constraint doing regulatory work is the most persuasive thing I can put in front of a skeptical partner. |
| ***Ziyārat al-marīḍ*** — the practice of visiting the sick | Physical presence with the patient is itself part of the treatment | **Home-based primary care / hospital-at-home.** Reimbursable, evidence-supported, and independently justified by measurement quality — see white-coat hypertension. In nursing homes the absence of visitation is a documented driver of decline, and almost no one is measuring it. |
| ***Iḥsān*** — excellence, doing more than the minimum owed | Duty of care exceeding the enforceable floor | **Continuity-of-care as risk management.** A physician the patient trusts is sued less. This is an underwriting argument and a malpractice-premium argument before it is an ethical one. |
| **Three noble professions** — teaching, law, medicine as vocations with obligations beyond price | Professional duty is not exhausted by the fee | **Deliberate positioning.** Teachers teach underpaid; lawyers take pro bono cases as a matter of professional norm; medicine has drifted. The venture reclaims that norm as a *market position* — the practice that sells the relationship other practices cannot afford to offer. |

**The line to hold, if a partner pushes.** None of this asks the firm to accept a religious premise. It asks the firm to recognize that a religious-ethical system produced a set of commercial constraints — no interest, no risk-bearing without defined consideration, no spending without needs evidence, local before distant, service as status — that a conservative structuring lawyer would largely recommend anyway. **The doctrine is the source of the constraints, not a substitute for the legal analysis.** The legal analysis is what I am retaining counsel to do.
