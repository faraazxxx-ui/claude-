Part 1The Objective, Restated Correctly

This is the part that must be conveyed to counsel first, because it inverts how a civil rights case is normally valued and therefore how it should be litigated.

What the client is actually buying

Not damages. A judicial finding that the prosecution was malicious in its origin. Three consequences follow, and each is worth more than the money:

Immigration An arrest and prosecution appear in the immigration record regardless of outcome. "Acquitted" still reads as "was charged." A civil finding that the charge was fabricated recharacterises the event itself — from a defendant who prevailed into a victim of bad-faith actors. That distinction is material before USCIS, and the client's status turned on employment the prosecution made unobtainable.
The three-year gap Every credentialing application, licensing board, and hiring committee asks what he was doing. "I was on trial" is damaging whatever the verdict. "I was the target of a malicious prosecution, I sued, and a court so found" is a different answer to the same question. The claim is a reputational instrument before it is a damages claim.
Conspiracy A malicious prosecution finding converts the criminal case from unrelated misfortune into an act within the retaliation campaign already pleaded in Rahman v. UHS. That is the single most valuable thing this action can do for the employment case — and see Count III, which supplies the doctrinal bridge.
The correction that matters, and it works in your favour

§ 1983 fabrication of evidence and § 1983 malicious prosecution are different claims with different elements, and only one of them produces the word you need. A fabrication verdict establishes that evidence was manufactured. It does not, of itself, establish "malicious prosecution."

So the fabrication count against the District Attorney — which you asked for and which appears below as Count II — is not the count that delivers your objective. Count I delivers it, and it runs against the officers, not the prosecutor. You can have both. But if only one survives, you want Count I. Plead them together and do not let counsel drop Count I as duplicative.

The one factual question that decides whether the DA count exists

Your statement video reportedly shows the complainant being coached twice — by the officer present, and by a person on the telephone during the statement.

Identify the voice on the telephone

That identity determines the entire defendant structure. If it was a prosecutor or a District Attorney's investigator directing the content of a complainant's statement before probable cause existed, that is squarely Zahrey and Buckley territory and Count II is live. If it was a family member or a UHS physician, there is no DA count — but Count III becomes far stronger, because a private party directing a police statement is a private actor jointly engaged with state officials. Either answer is useful. The video already contains it. This is the first thing to put in front of counsel.

Part 3Draft Count Language

For counsel to redraft. Bracketed items require factual confirmation. Paragraph numbering assumes a general-allegations section precedes.

§ 1983 Malicious Prosecution — the count that delivers the objective

COUNT I — DEPRIVATION OF LIBERTY WITHOUT PROBABLE CAUSE (MALICIOUS PROSECUTION), 42 U.S.C. § 1983, FOURTH AND FOURTEENTH AMENDMENTS
Against Defendants [Officer 1], [Officer 2], and John Does 1–5

1. Defendants, acting under colour of state law, initiated and continued a criminal proceeding against Plaintiff, a licensed physician then completing residency training, by procuring and forwarding to the prosecuting authority a complainant's statement they knew to be false.

2. Defendants lacked probable cause. The statement on which the charge rested was not the complainant's independent account. It was shaped in real time by Defendants and by [the person on the telephone], as the recorded statement itself demonstrates.

3. Defendants acted with actual malice. Defendants knew or recklessly disregarded that the complaint was filed the day after the complainant's parents learned of her relationship with Plaintiff; that the complainant had been voluntarily present at Plaintiff's residence [the following day, as photographic evidence establishes]; and that a witness present the preceding evening would contradict the account.

4. The criminal proceeding terminated in Plaintiff's favour. On [DATE] August 2025, after trial, Plaintiff was found NOT GUILTY in Binghamton City Court. Under Thompson v. Clark, 596 U.S. 36 (2022), a plaintiff need show only that the prosecution ended without a conviction; an acquittal at trial is the strongest available form of favourable termination.

5. Plaintiff suffered a deprivation of liberty consequent on the legal process — arrest, arraignment, conditions of release, and compelled appearance over approximately seventeen months.

6. As a direct and proximate result, Plaintiff lost a contracted position at $300,000 per annum, was unable to obtain licensure or employment for the duration of the prosecution, suffered injury to his professional reputation and to his immigration position, and incurred defence costs and medical expense — all while carrying an implanted cardiac defibrillator placed in February 2024.

§ 1983 Fabrication of Evidence — the count against prosecutorial investigative conduct

COUNT II — FABRICATION OF EVIDENCE AND DENIAL OF THE RIGHT TO A FAIR TRIAL, 42 U.S.C. § 1983
Against Defendants [Officer 1], [Officer 2], and [Prosecutorial Actor], the latter solely in respect of conduct performed in an investigative capacity

7. Plaintiff has a clearly established constitutional right not to be deprived of liberty on the basis of evidence fabricated by a government official. Zahrey v. Coffey, 221 F.3d 342 (2d Cir. 2000); Ricciuti v. N.Y.C. Transit Auth., 124 F.3d 123 (2d Cir. 1997).

8. Defendants created false evidence — a complainant's statement whose content they supplied, directed, or materially altered — and forwarded it to the prosecuting authority, where it was likely to influence and did influence the decision to charge and the conduct of the proceeding.

9. The conduct alleged against [Prosecutorial Actor] was investigative, not advocative. It occurred before any charging decision had been made and consisted of searching for and manufacturing corroboration that might supply probable cause. Such conduct is not protected by absolute immunity. Buckley v. Fitzsimmons, 509 U.S. 259, 273 (1993) (a prosecutor "searching for the clues and corroboration that might give him probable cause to recommend that a suspect be arrested" performs a function no different from that of a detective); Zahrey, 221 F.3d at 349–57.

10. Plaintiff expressly does not challenge, and asserts no claim arising from, any decision to initiate or maintain the prosecution, or any conduct in the presentation of the State's case. Plaintiff acknowledges that such conduct is absolutely immune under Imbler v. Pachtman, 424 U.S. 409 (1976), and pleads no count in respect of it.

11. This claim did not accrue until the criminal proceeding terminated in Plaintiff's favour in August 2025. McDonough v. Smith, 588 U.S. 109 (2019).

Note on paragraph 10. It is doing real work. It tells the court, on the face of the pleading, that counsel knows where the immunity line sits and has drawn the claim on the correct side of it. That is the difference between a fabrication claim that survives and one that reads as a disguised attack on charging discretion.

§ 1983 Conspiracy — the bridge to the employment case

COUNT III — CONSPIRACY TO DEPRIVE PLAINTIFF OF CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS, 42 U.S.C. § 1983
Against all Defendants, including the private Defendants

12. Defendants, state and private, reached an agreement to procure Plaintiff's arrest and prosecution on a charge they knew to be false, and committed overt acts in furtherance of it, resulting in the deprivations alleged above.

13. The private Defendants acted under colour of state law. A private party who is a willing participant in joint action with state officials acts under colour of law for § 1983 purposes. Dennis v. Sparks, 449 U.S. 24, 27–28 (1980); Adickes v. S.H. Kress & Co., 398 U.S. 144 (1970).

14. The agreement is evidenced by, among other things: [the recorded admission that the accusation was false and that Plaintiff had been framed]; the direction of the complainant's statement by a person on the telephone during its taking; body-camera references to contacting physicians at United Health Services; the disclosure of Plaintiff's protected medical and academic information to the complainant's family; and the timing of the complaint, filed the day after that family learned of the relationship.

15. The conspiracy was a continuation of, and in furtherance of, the campaign of retaliation pleaded in Rahman v. United Health Services Hospitals, Inc., No. 3:26-cv-00197 (N.D.N.Y.).

Declaratory relief — the part that serves the actual objective

COUNT VII — DECLARATORY JUDGMENT, 28 U.S.C. §§ 2201–2202

16. Plaintiff seeks a declaration that the criminal proceeding commenced against him in April 2024 was instituted and maintained without probable cause and with actual malice, and that the evidence on which it rested was fabricated. Plaintiff's professional licensure, hospital credentialing, and immigration position each require him to account for the proceeding; a declaration is necessary to prevent its continued use against him and constitutes relief distinct from, and not redressable by, damages.

Ask counsel candidly whether Count VII is viable — declaratory relief as to past conduct is sometimes held redundant of the damages claim. If it will not stand as a count, the same objective can be secured through requested findings of fact, a special verdict form, or settlement recitals. What matters is that the maliciousness ends up on the record in a document you can hand to a credentialing committee, by whichever mechanism survives.

Part 5The Mandate To Counsel

This is the section to read aloud on the first call. It is what distinguishes this engagement from an ordinary contingency case, and a lawyer who does not accept it is the wrong lawyer however good they are.

"I will trade dollars for findings"

A contingency practice is structured to maximise settlement value. That incentive is misaligned with this client's objective. A quiet six-figure settlement with a confidentiality clause and a non-admission provision would pay the firm and destroy the thing the client came for — because he cannot hand a confidential non-admission to a credentialing committee or to USCIS.

State this at the outset, in the engagement letter:

  • No confidentiality clause covering the facts. The client must remain free to describe the prosecution as fabricated.
  • No non-admission clause as to maliciousness — or, if unavoidable, settlement recitals that state the absence of probable cause as agreed fact.
  • Willingness to try the case. If the finding is the remedy, a case that always settles never delivers it. Ask directly: "When did you last try a § 1983 case to verdict?"
  • Count I is not to be dropped as duplicative of Count II. It is the count that produces the word.

The honest summary

The count you asked for is real, viable, and supported by binding Second Circuit authority — Zahrey is directly on point and I underweighted it in earlier analysis. But it is the hardest count in the complaint, it depends on a fact you have not yet confirmed, and it is not the count that gives you the word "malicious." Count I does that, against the officers, on an acquittal that satisfies Thompson v. Clark comfortably. Plead them together, lead with Count I, and hold the engagement terms in Part 5 — because the way this objective is most likely to be lost is not a dismissal. It is a quiet settlement that pays you and says nothing.
