Understanding your kidney test results

Renal Tubular Acidosis

Your recent blood and urine tests point to a kidney condition called renal tubular acidosis, or RTA. This sheet explains what that means, why it causes kidney stones and blood in your urine, and what happens next.

What we found

  • Slightly too much acid in your blood. A substance called bicarbonate soaks up acid the way a sponge soaks up water. Yours is a little low.
  • Your urine is not acidic enough. Healthy kidneys push spare acid out into the urine. Yours are not doing that as well as they should.
  • A small kidney stone, with blood in your urine and pain in your side.

What RTA is

Picture your kidneys as millions of tiny drains. Each one pulls back what your body needs and pushes waste — including acid — out into your urine. In RTA, the last part of the drain has trouble pushing the acid out.

Starts here
The drain cannot push out enough acid
So
Acid builds up in your blood
So
Your bones release calcium to neutralize it
Result
Calcium forms stones — causing pain and bleeding

These are not four separate problems. They are one chain — which is good news, because fixing the first link improves all of them.

Why it happened

Finding the cause is part of what we are doing now. The three we check for most often:

  • An autoimmune condition, where the immune system mistakenly targets healthy tissue. The most common one here also causes dry eyes and a dry mouth — please tell us if you have either.
  • A medicine you are taking. Some seizure and migraine medicines cause exactly this. Bring every medicine, vitamin, and supplement to your next visit, including anything you buy without a prescription.
  • The way your kidneys are built. Some people are born with a variation that produces this pattern.

Your bladder infection has been treated and cured. It is not the cause of the acid problem — but infections and stones do feed each other, so we take both seriously.

Please strain your urine — every time

We will give you a small strainer. Use it at every visit to the toilet and bring us anything you catch, even a speck. Testing what a stone is made of tells us more than almost any other test we can run. It is quick, it costs nothing, and it can change your whole treatment.

How it is treated

The main treatment is a medicine called potassium citrate. It does two jobs at once: it neutralizes the extra acid, and the citrate coats calcium in your urine so it cannot clump into stones. Alongside it:

  • Drink enough to pass about 2.5 litres of urine a day. Spread it across the day, including some before bed.
  • Cut back on salt. Salt pulls calcium into your urine and makes stones more likely.
  • Go easy on large amounts of meat and protein.
  • Keep eating normal amounts of dairy with your meals. This surprises people — but calcium in food actually protects you, because it traps stone-forming material in your gut before it ever reaches your kidneys. Do not cut out dairy.
  • Avoid calcium pills between meals unless we prescribe them. They work differently from calcium in food and can make stones worse.
  • Use only pasteurized milk and cheese. Cheese made from raw milk can carry the kind of bacteria that caused your infection.

Call us the same day if you have

  • Muscle weakness, or trouble lifting your arms or standing up from a chair
  • A fast, pounding, or irregular heartbeat
  • Fever with chills, along with back or side pain
  • Passing no urine, or far less than usual
  • Severe pain your medicine will not control, or heavy bleeding and clots

The bottom line

This is a manageable condition. Once the acid level is corrected, stones usually slow down or stop, the bleeding and pain settle, and your bones are protected. Most people do well on one daily medicine and a few changes to what they drink and eat.
