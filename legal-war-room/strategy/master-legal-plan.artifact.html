2. The Legal Story (cold open — verbatim from legal-story.md)

He came to this country to be a doctor, and he became a good one — good enough that when his own hospital tried to put him on probation, the current head of medical education wrote that it was “very inhumane for you to put this kid on probation… he’s a good doctor” (00:26:23). He is also a cardiac patient: Brugada syndrome, an implanted defibrillator that is supposed to be checked every four months (00:32:39).

On April 1, by his account, he signed a three-year Florida attending contract — $350,000 salary plus $70,000 bonus (DOCUMENTED-PENDING / ASSERTED — executed contract not yet in counsel’s possession; see OPEN FACTUAL FLAG 2) (00:13:03). Thirteen days later, on April 14, UHS placed him on probation without telling him (00:14:21) — he and his treating physician learned of it only afterward (“He didn’t know I was on probation. I didn’t know I was on probation,” 00:26:23) — on paperwork he alleges carries a forged signature UHS itself possesses (00:56:56). That same day he invoked FMLA (00:14:21, 00:43:40). And on that same April 14 — the canon’s date for the arrest-day events (00:42:37; see OPEN FACTUAL FLAG 3) — on the day he was scheduled to fly down for the Florida interview (00:14:21), he was arrested: police were waiting at his car as he went to get his laptop to write his FMLA document (00:14:21, 00:55:54). In May, a Vermont offer was “taken away” (00:13:03). The call to police came from Guthrie, not from the complainant (00:40:05). His photograph went up “all across the hospital — if you see this person please call the police” (00:42:37); the poster spread inside UHS until a nurse messaged him, “is this you?” (01:08:55).

Then the institutions told on themselves. Within roughly 24 hours the administrative leave was lifted, every internal investigation at both hospitals was dropped, and UHS asked him to come back to work (00:43:40). Though its own rules made an arrest on probation immediately fireable, UHS — on a decision made by its corporate legal team, not the program (00:47:06, 00:50:15) — kept him, and graduated him (00:45:36). “You knew I was not guilty before anybody else did” (00:54:36). Yet UHS then handed the DA his academic records and cut off his email (00:45:36, 00:51:14), and the prosecution ran on for more than a year. He refused a diversion deal because USCIS “go like, well, if you were not guilty why you take the course” (01:03:33). In September 2025 a jury found him NOT GUILTY — favorable termination confirmed by counsel (01:02:46).

The acquittal did not give him his life back. The licensing questionnaire asks “have you ever been arrested — not charged, not acquitted, just arrested” (01:01:30). Three immigration attorneys say the arrest record endangers his status despite acquittal (01:02:46). He still cannot pick up his medical license (00:33:46). He has not practiced medicine for three years (01:03:33). His family has spent close to $1,000,000 in documentable living expenses (ESTIMABLE — bank records, 00:32:39), his father wiring ~$300,000/year structured as loans (ESTIMABLE — bank statements, 00:28:43), while he went without a $4,000-a-bottle essential medication (ASSERTED, 00:27:22) and his defibrillator went three years unchecked (00:32:39).

This lawsuit is the only instrument that can repair the record, reopen the licensure path, and give USCIS a truthful narrative. The ask is not vengeance. It is accountability, record repair, and making sure this cannot happen to the next resident. We are on the right side of this conversation — the goal is accountability and sustainable change, not punishment.
